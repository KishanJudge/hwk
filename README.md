# Feminism in the Era of Thatcherism, 1979-1999: Worksheet Answers

Source: *Women and the Women's Movement in Britain*, Chapter 12, pp.334-353. Figures are exactly as printed. Quotes are used only where the wording matters; the rest is paraphrased.

## Impact of Thatcher's appointment (pp.334-337)

**Thatcher as a feminist?** No.

- Her rise to party leader in 1976 and PM in 1979 was the climax of female advance in twentieth-century Britain, and she benefited from gains made by earlier generations of women.
- She refused to acknowledge any debt to them. She believed she owed her success to her own talents and hard work, and thought women should stop complaining and use the opportunities already open to them.
- In power she behaved like a male executive and avoided association with other women. No woman except briefly Lady Young entered her cabinet, and the careers of several able Tory women suffered a check.
- Her policies seemed unfavourable to women. Separate taxation (Nigel Lawson) was the only obvious gain. Child benefits were frozen, and the government resisted pressure to improve maternity leave, where Britain lagged far behind other western countries.
- Hence Spare Rib's 1979 comment: 'one prime minister doesn't make a matriarchy'.

**Thatcher as a role model?** Partly, and unintentionally.

- She did nothing directly to promote women in politics, but the 1980s and 1990s saw female role models in new fields: Stella Rimington (Crown Prosecution Service), Betty Boothroyd (Speaker of the House of Commons), Rosie Boycott (editor of *The Independent* and the *Daily Express*), Elizabeth Butler Sloss (Lord Justice of Appeal) and Nicola Horlick (City of London).
- She finally killed off the idea that women had no aptitude for politics. She won in 1983 and 1987, and the party's rank and file regarded her as their greatest asset.
- The long premiership gave younger women the knowledge that their country was dominated by a woman. Natasha Walters regarded her almost as an unsung heroine.
- The author calls her "in spite of herself" a radicalising force for British women, though a dilemma for feminists. Many felt some admiration, refused to support 'Ditch the Bitch' campaigns, and felt a certain *schadenfreude* at her treatment of male colleagues.
- Her galvanising effect was greatest on Labour women, not Conservative ones, and wasn't fully appreciated until the mid-1990s.

## Feminism and anti-militarism, peace camps: how effective? (pp.337-338)

**Origins**

- The women's peace movement had dwindled in the 1970s, then revived around the Greenham Common peace camp at Newbury, Berkshire, from 1980-1.
- Women felt women's liberation was perpetuating the male value system on violence, and were unhappy about the campaign for equality in the armed forces. Above all, nuclear accidents (the Soviet Union, America, Windscale in Cumbria) undermined official claims that nuclear power was safe.
- In October 1979 it was announced that 140 Cruise Missiles would be stationed in Britain. The Ministry of Defence then announced that ninety-six would go to Greenham Common, and a campaign was launched by Joan Ruddock, a local Labour Party activist and Citizens Advice Bureau worker.

**Effectiveness**

- In summer 1981 women marched from Cardiff to Greenham Common under the banner 'Women for Life on Earth', a spontaneous eco-feminism.
- The camp recaptured the élan and tactics of the Edwardian suffragettes. Women chained themselves to the perimeter fence, fixed domestic artefacts to it, encircled the whole base as a human chain, cut the wire and entered the base, and kept camping despite eviction orders.
- By 1982 the camp had a high profile, maintained by court cases leading to sentences in Holloway.

**Limitations**

- Some feminists disagreed. Some saw it as little more feminist than Women's Institutes, as too biased towards maternalism, and as a symptom of the decline of women's liberation.
- Organised feminism was slow to take up the issue, and the women-only groups were seen as divisive within the peace movement.
- The government stubbornly maintained its policy.

**Impact**

- The camps continued through the 1980s.
- Polls showed a higher proportion of women than men opposed both nuclear weapons and the nuclear industry. This helped bring about a broader change in women's political stance, evident in the 1990s.
- It accelerated the shift from Socialist feminism towards an emphasis on gender difference and the positive celebration of a woman-centred culture.

## Marriage figures (p.339)

- The marriage rate peaked in the early 1970s and fell by about half between 1971 and 1991.
- Marriage was largely replaced by cohabitation, which increased ninefold, from 3 to 26 per cent among single women aged 20-49 between 1978 and 1993. It became almost usual to cohabit before marriage.
- The old double standard in sexual relations greatly diminished.

## 1981 Women Against Violence Against Women (p.339)

- A conference of eight hundred women organised by WAVAW helped focus the debate on violence against women.
- Feminists regularly attacked judges who treated offences involving rape too lightly.
- The House of Lords ruling on marital rape in 1981 marked an important victory.
- Women continued to be victims of violence within and outside marriage.

## Average age for giving birth (p.339)

- By 1990 the average age for giving birth had risen to twenty-seven years, and to twenty-five for the first child.
- It was estimated that 21 per cent of all women born in 1965 would remain childless.
- Statisticians therefore expected Britain's population to begin to fall for the first time since the Black Death.

## Divorce figures (p.340)

- By the 1990s over 150,000 couples divorced each year, and nearly four in ten marriages were expected to end in divorce.
- In 1971, 7 per cent of families with dependent children were headed by a lone mother. By 1994 it was 20 per cent.
- Only two-fifths of these cases arose from divorce. The rest mostly resulted from a threefold increase in births outside marriage between 1978 and 1993, to 30 per cent of the total.
- Public debate linked divorce with the single-parent family and the alleged collapse of parenting skills. Women were made to bear the responsibility for delinquency and criminality amongst the young, as in the 1950s.
- The traditional two-parent family remained the norm.

## Changes in the labour market (pp.340-341)

- Between 1979 and 1994 three million, largely male, jobs disappeared from British industry.
- The official unemployment rate of 10.3 per cent in February 1994 concealed a male rate of 13.2 per cent and a female rate of only 5.4 per cent.
- Women in paid employment rose from 44 per cent in 1971 to 53 per cent in 1994. Among women aged 25-44 it rose from 52 per cent in 1971 to 68 per cent by 1987.
- The 2.3 million increase in the labour force between 1971 and 1987 was almost wholly accounted for by women. By the late 1990s women exceeded men in the labour force for the first time.
- The Department of Employment expected nine out of ten new jobs by the end of the 1990s to be filled by women.
- Causes included the collapse of manufacturing and the expansion of the service industries, and changing attitudes. More qualified girls applied to university, and graduates were more positive about employment. In 1994 the Association of Graduate Careers Advisory Services reported that women displayed a better attitude at interviews.
- Working mothers became accepted. In 1990, 41 per cent of mothers with children under five had paid employment, compared with 23 per cent in 1983. The post-1997 Labour Government put pressure on single mothers to take up paid work.

## Glass ceiling (p.342)

**Pay**

- By 1990 women earned 71 per cent of male income in manual occupations and 63 per cent in non-manual ones.
- The overall figure of 68 per cent compared unfavourably with France and Germany at 80 per cent and Denmark and Italy at 85 per cent.

**Causes**

- Women's concentration in part-time employment (four out of five such workers) and their different age structure explained some of it, but not all. Stubborn prejudice and male control also hampered them.

**Professions in 1990**

- Women were barely 8 per cent of architects, 10 per cent of chartered accountants, 23 per cent of dentists, 21 per cent of solicitors and 26 per cent of vets.
- By 1995 women outnumbered men among solicitors under thirty, and more women than men were being admitted as vets.

**Medicine**

- Almost half of medical students were female by 1990, but many dropped out because of long hours and discrimination against married women with children.
- Women were only 22 per cent of GPs, barely 15 per cent of hospital consultants and only 12 per cent of consultants even in obstetrics and gynaecology.
- In 1986 only six out of 957 consultant posts in surgery in England and Wales were held by women.

**Responses**

- John Major launched 'Opportunity 2000' in 1991 to improve women's position in senior public appointments, but had no women in his cabinet, so his support carried little credibility.
- Child care became a priority, but local authority provision remained extremely limited, which deterred women in low-paid jobs.
- Women also bore the brunt of domesticity. A late-1980s survey found domestic tasks done mainly by women in 72 per cent of households and shared equally in 22 per cent. A 1993 survey found women did 66 per cent of the shopping, 75 per cent of the cleaning and 77 per cent of the cooking, though this still reflected significant reductions in women's burden.

## Changes to abortion law (p.344)

- The Society for the Protection of the Unborn Child, backed by MPs John Corrie, David Alton and Anne Widdecombe, repeatedly tried to amend the 1967 Act.
- Corrie's 1979 Bill was defeated, even in a Conservative House of Commons.
- In 1981 the Department of Health and Social Security modified the grounds. The decision was to be based on which a doctor could perform an abortion on medical grounds alone, not social ones. A million abortions had been performed since 1967 on social grounds, so this appeared a major threat.
- However, the ruling was not generally put into practice, because the medical profession and MPs had largely come to accept the existing law. The anti-feminists now represented a vocal minority.
- The author sees this as part of a general shift of attitudes towards the feminist position. By the 1990s abortion and one-parent families were taken for granted by most people.

## Reaction of men and the Men's Movement, incl. boys' underperformance (pp.345-347)

**Grievances**

- Men were widely thought to be losing their role as breadwinners.
- Male suicide rates soared, and boys were 'underperforming' in schools.
- There was a shortage of male teachers in primary schools.
- The Child Support Agency was turning men into taxpayers without rights.
- Health resources went to female problems like breast cancer while prostate cancer was neglected.
- Husbands were being criminalised by the House of Lords ruling on marital rape.
- The thesis was that the Establishment had fallen under the thumb of the feminist lobby and women's liberation had gone too far. A 'Men's Movement' emerged in the 1980s.

**Organisations and writing**

- The 1990s saw pressure groups such as Families Need Fathers, Dads After Divorce and the Campaign for Justice in Divorce.
- There was also the quarterly magazine *Male View*, a 'Men In Crisis' helpline, and Neil Lyndon's book *No More Sex War* (1992).
- Some prominent women backed the thesis. Novelist Fay Weldon blamed feminism for disheartened, under-achieving men, and journalist Melanie Phillips argued men needed paid work more urgently than single mothers.

**Boys' underperformance**

- By 1997 government surveys suggested two-thirds of fourteen-year-old girls attained the expected levels, whereas only half of boys did.
- At 'O' level stage it was once boys who had overtaken girls. Now 48 per cent of girls achieved five good GCSE passes compared with only 39 per cent of boys.
- The result was a group of young men who, for lack of qualifications, were likely to become almost unemployable. This led to high crime rates and a soaring suicide rate amongst young men.

**Middle-aged men**

- Four out of ten men over fifty-five but below retirement age had given up work and had no expectations of further employment.
- By 1995, 20 per cent of men had female partners who were the main breadwinners, three times as many as in 1980.

**Overall verdict**

- The author argues that gender relations were being re-adjusted, rather than completely overthrown, and that many middle-aged men welcomed early retirement.
- The surprising thing is how limited and how ineffective the reaction against feminism was.
